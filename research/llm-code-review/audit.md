# Contradictions (ranked)

## C1. What is the true precision / false-positive burden of LLM-generated review comments?
- side A (very low FP / high precision — vendor & one vendor-internal shop): V-S3 "Reviewer has achieved a &lt;3% false-positive rate across tens of thousands of code changes reviewed."; V-S4 "Diamond has achieved a 96% positive feedback rate on AI-generated comments"; P-S9 "Engineers largely agree with what it surfaces: less than 1% of findings are marked incorrect."; V-S2 "Bugbot detects the hardest logic bugs with a low false positive rate."
- side B (high noise / low precision — independent benches, live orgs, practitioners): K-S1 "A primary factor limiting higher F1 scores for all techniques is their low precision, indicative of a high false positive rate. [...] other four ACR techniques, all of which exhibited precision scores below 10%."; K-S3 "8.1% and 7.2%, respectively, of LLM-generated comments were accepted by reviewers"; P-S8 "~19% were good, 2% were flat-out incorrect, and 79% were nits"; V-S10/P-S5 (CodeRabbit restating Martian online) "49.2% precision means roughly one in two comments leads to a code change"; V-S9/P-S3 online framing still leaves large un-acted share even for high-recall tools; P-S1 "Only about 1 in 5 comments were actually good catches"; P-S2 "Every single time, the code review comments the AI bot leaves on my pull requests are not just wrong, but laughably wrong."; K-S7 "Sometimes the mistakes it thinks it finds are not mistakes at all."
- better evidenced: **Side B for industry-at-large / multi-tool picture**; side A is primary only as *self-measured product metrics* with conflict of interest, non-shared definitions of “false positive,” and no shared gold defect oracle. Side B includes multi-org live acceptance (RevMate), multi-tool offline F1 (SWR-Bench), pre-filter comment audits (Greptile’s own engineering honesty), and independent Martian online act-on rates (~50% precision even for “winning” tools). Anthropic’s &lt;1% “incorrect” is primary for *one* internal deployment under their feedback process, not a general law.
- resolvable: **partially** — corpus supports “precision is highly tool-, tuning-, and metric-definition-dependent, and independent multi-tool evidence clusters well below vendor FP claims.” Not fully resolvable without a shared FP definition and independent multi-tool study with defect oracles (corpus lacks that single design).

## C2. Absolute defect-finding power: what fraction of real bugs do LLM reviewers catch?
- side A (high catch on vendor benches): V-S7 "Greptile led with an 82% catch rate, 41% higher than Bugbot (58%). [...] CodeRabbit at 44%, and Graphite at 6%."; V-S8 "Augment Code Review | 65% | 55% | 59%" (precision/recall/F on own golden-comment bench); V-S1 "resolution rate from 52% to over 70%" / "~0.5" resolved bugs per PR.
- side B (materially lower ceiling / different ranking): V-S9/K-S6/P-S3 "no tool found more than 63% of the known issues. The best tool still missed a third of the bugs."; V-S9 notes offline gold started from Augment/Greptile datasets ("may be biased toward the kinds of issues those tools prioritize"); K-S1 top automated F1 only ~19% on SWR-Bench; A-S1 correctness classification only ~64–69% vs unit-test ground truth; K-S2 ChatGPT matches only ~10% of human-reported quality issues.
- better evidenced: **Side B’s ceiling claim (≤~63% offline known issues; large miss rate) is better as a cross-tool upper bound** because Martian is the only multi-tool bench that is *not* self-ranking the publisher’s product first, and it explicitly flags vendor-bench bias. Greptile 82% and Augment 55% recall are primary measurements of *their own* eval sets but are secondary as evidence of field-wide effectiveness (COI + non-comparable protocols). SWR-Bench and unit-test classification are primary but measure different tasks (issue coverage F1 / correctness gate ≠ PR bug recall).
- resolvable: **no** — tool rankings and absolute catch rates conflict across protocols; corpus cannot pick a single “true” defect-find rate vs humans without a shared, non-vendor gold set scored for both LLM tools *and* human reviewers on the same PRs.

## C3. Do LLM review comments save review time / reduce human defect-finding work, or add net load?
- side A (saves effort / catches earlier so humans do less nit work): V-S6 "These changes help developers catch more issues earlier and improve code quality with less manual effort."; V-S3 "allowing them to squash bugs in their PRs before teammates even start their review."; P-S7 "Self reviews raised the floor: [...] cutting out back-and-forth by roughly a third."; V-S5 frames positive-feedback lift as "improved quality assurance."
- side B (does not replace humans; can slow merge; humans still must validate): K-S2 "should not be considered as a valid alternative to them nor as a mean to save code review time, since human reviewers would still need to perform their manual inspection while also validating the quality issues reported by the DL-based technique."; K-S7 "overall average pull request closure duration increased from five hours 52 minutes to eight hours 20 minutes" and human comment volume drop "was not statistically significant"; A-S4 "We did not detect any statistically significant change in any of the following: total duration of code reviews, time developers actively spent on the code review, the number of comment-response iterations"; P-S2 "These contextless, pretend code reviews are wasting my time."; P-S12 "false positives make noise:signal harder and slower to parse."
- better evidenced: **Side B for “does not replace humans / does not reliably shrink review cost.”** Google AutoCommenter A/B (A-S4) and Beko pre/post (K-S7) are primary industrial measurements with before/after or experimental design; ICPC human-vs-ChatGPT study (K-S2) is primary controlled PR comparison. Side A is mostly vendor framing or qualitative interview synthesis (P-S7 self-review nits), not controlled defect-or-time RCTs against human-only review.
- resolvable: **yes for the narrow claim “LLM review is not a drop-in time saver that removes human review”** (corpus agrees across skeptic + Google A/B + industry deployment). **no for net ROI** (time cost of FP triage vs escaped-defect value) — not measured with a common cost model in the corpus.

## C4. Are high “resolved/accepted” rates evidence of finding real defects?
- side A (behavioral resolution ≈ valuable real issues): V-S1 "resolution rate. It uses AI to determine, at PR merge time, which bugs were actually resolved by the author"; V-S4 "67% implementation rate of suggested changes, indicating that developers find the feedback actionable and valuable"; P-S6 "In 71% of the reviews, Copilot code review surfaces actionable feedback"; P-S8 address rate "19% to 55+%" after noise filtering treated as success metric.
- side B (labels/acceptance inflate or diverge from real defect detection): K-S3 "Refactoring-related comments are more likely to be accepted than Functional comments (18.2% and 18.6% compared to 4.8% and 5.2%)" while most generated comments are classified functional; K-S7 "73.8% of automated code review comments were labeled as resolved" *while* closure time rose and authors report non-mistakes; V-S9 "online and offline benchmarks disagree" and incomplete gold sets understate precision / overstate recall; K-S1/K-S2/A-S2 reject BLEU/EM and warn acceptance ≠ issue coverage; P-S8’s own audit: 79% nits that are "technically true but not something the dev cared about" before filtering.
- better evidenced: **Side B on construct validity** — primary multi-org evidence that acceptance is higher for style/refactor than functional defects (K-S3), and that “resolved” can coexist with longer cycle time and irrelevant comments (K-S7). Side A metrics are useful product KPIs but are not validated in-corpus as defect-finding rates against independent oracles; Cursor even uses AI to judge “resolved.”
- resolvable: **yes** that acceptance/resolution is a biased proxy (corpus is consistent). **no** for a calibrated mapping from acceptance% → true-positive defect rate.

## C5. Can LLM reviewers match or exceed human reviewers at finding the defects humans care about?
- side A (anecdotal / marketing “catches what humans miss”): V-S2 "I've seen it catch implementation errors that received a pass of human code review."; V-S2 "Bugbot finds real bugs after human approval."; P-S9 "The change would have broken authentication [...] the engineer shared afterwards that they wouldn't have caught it on their own."; V-S10 "CodeRabbit finds more real bugs than anyone else" (*among tools*, not vs humans).
- side B (low overlap with human issues; moderate correctness judgment; not replacement): K-S2 "it can only spot 10% of the quality issues reported by humans" while recommending ~2.4× more changes; A-S2 "ChatGPT succeeds in 19/150=13% of cases" on code-to-comment vs human-like comments; A-S1 "fully automated code review process may be unreliable" at ~68.5% correctness classification; A-S4 AutoCommenter useful ratio 60% vs 80% target, ~40% resolution, best-practice scope not general defects; A-S8 human–model α ~0.15 vs human–human 0.80 on static-analysis warning actionability; P-S10 "the most effective guardrail against slop is extremely talented engineers doing very thoughtful, human code review."
- better evidenced: **Side B for systematic comparison to humans.** Only K-S2 and related academic work (A-S2, A-S1, A-S4, A-S8) directly measure overlap with human judgments or executable/or human gold labels. Side A is testimonials and vendor comparative tool leaderboards — primary as anecdotes, weak as general effectiveness vs humans.
- resolvable: **yes directionally** — corpus does **not** support LLM superiority or parity with humans on real-defect finding; best controlled evidence shows low overlap with human issues and moderate correctness accuracy. **no** for a quantified “LLM finds X% of defects humans find / humans find Y% LLMs find” multi-site RCT (absent).

## C6. Are vendor self-benchmarks trustworthy indicators of relative tool quality?
- side A (treat self-benches as real rankings): V-S7 Greptile #1 at 82% catch; V-S8 Augment #1 at 59% F; V-S4 Graphite claims competitors (CodeRabbit) have highest FP.
- side B (self-benches biased; independent multi-tool picture differs): V-S9 "vendor-published (well-resourced but potentially biased toward the vendor's tool)"; V-S9 offline gold "started with Augment and Greptile's previously published datasets"; V-S9/P-S3 online vs offline rankings disagree; K-S6 same Martian caveats; tool order flips across V-S7 vs V-S8 vs Martian online (CodeRabbit strong online recall) vs Graphite high precision/low recall.
- better evidenced: **Side B.** Even the vendor corpus’s own independent bench (Martian) warns against treating vendor-published rankings as ground truth, and cross-bench rank instability is documented in-corpus.
- resolvable: **yes** — for research synthesis, vendor self-wins should get effective weight far below independent multi-tool benches and live multi-org studies.

# Gaps

- No peer-reviewed (or otherwise controlled) head-to-head where **human reviewers and LLM reviewers** review the **same PRs**, scored against an **independent defect oracle** (not human comments, not author acceptance), reporting precision, recall, and complementary find sets.
- No multi-site measurement of **production defect escape rate** (bugs that ship) under human-only vs human+LLM vs LLM-first review policies.
- No shared, standardized definition of **false positive** (wrong claim vs nit vs out-of-scope vs already-acceptable tradeoff) applied across tools.
- No calibrated conversion from **acceptance / resolution / thumbs-up** to true-positive **defect** detection (authors may “resolve” nits, delete code paths, or comply with confident-wrong bots).
- Incomplete quantification of **gold-set incompleteness** magnitude (Martian flags the bias; corpus has no correction factors usable for synthesis).
- Little evidence on **defect class mix**: security, concurrency, distributed failure modes, performance regressions, vs style/API nits — broken out for LLM vs human catch rates.
- Sparse data on **senior vs junior human baselines**; “better than a tired human skim” ≠ “better than a careful reviewer.”
- Custom-instructions / org-tuning effects (P-S1, Greptile filters) are real but **not** meta-analyzed: how much of “effectiveness” is product defaults vs months of prompt/rule engineering?
- Cost model gap: engineer-minutes spent **triaging FP noise** vs expected value of true positives (including rare sev-class bugs).
- Generalizability: language, monorepo vs polyglot, proprietary vs OSS, regulated domains — corpus is GitHub/PR and a few firms (Google, Mozilla, Ubisoft, Beko, Anthropic) without systematic stratification.
- Temporal stability: model/product versions move monthly; few longitudinal studies after the Beko and AutoCommenter snapshots.
- Whether LLM review **harms** juniors via confident wrong advice (raised in P-S2) is unmeasured beyond anecdote.
- Independence of “online recall” from **who still has humans reading the bot** — if teams ignore noisy bots, act-on metrics collapse; selection into “teams that still read AI comments” is uncharacterized.
